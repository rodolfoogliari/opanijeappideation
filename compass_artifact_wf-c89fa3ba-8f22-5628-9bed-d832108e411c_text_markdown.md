# The Neuropsychology of Engagement Mechanics: An Evidence Base for a Constraint-Bound Percussion Practice App

## TL;DR
- The most *potent* engagement mechanics (variable-ratio rewards, streaks, leaderboards, loot-box-style uncertainty) work by exploiting dopaminergic "wanting," loss aversion and social comparison — but the peer-reviewed evidence shows their effects are **small-to-moderate, decay within 4–12 weeks (novelty effect), and can actively backfire** (amotivation, anxiety, gaming-the-metric, and in the gambling-adjacent cases, measurable harm). This product has forbidden most of them, and the literature suggests the retention cost is **real but smaller than practitioners assume**.
- For a single-player, judgement-free product, the strongest remaining evidence-backed levers are: **self-determination-theory need support** (autonomy/competence/relatedness), **self-monitoring/pure counting** (Harkin et al. 2016, d≈0.40), **implementation intentions / cue design** (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, d=0.65), **the intrinsic reward of the musical activity itself** (groove/entrainment/dopaminergic musical pleasure), and **relatedness via a real named master** (an "appointment" mechanic and recognition, which the music-dropout literature identifies as strongly protective).
- The rhythm/groove neuroscience is the strongest single asset: playing along to a pulse is *intrinsically* rewarding at the neural level, so this product needs extrinsic scaffolding **less** than a language app does — but the biggest blind spot is that need-supportive, low-extrinsic designs are worst exactly where this app is weakest (getting a wavering beginner *back* on day 2–7 with no notification pressure and no human contact between monthly cycles).

---

## Key Findings

1. **The "dopamine hit" framing of game design is a genuine oversimplification.** Dopamine encodes *reward-prediction error* and drives *"wanting"* (incentive salience), not *"liking"* (hedonic pleasure). Design that maximises "wanting" without "liking" is precisely the recipe for compulsive, low-satisfaction use — the opposite of durable, wellbeing-compatible engagement.
2. **Gamification works, but modestly and unevenly.** The best meta-analytic estimate for learning is g≈0.25–0.49 (Sailer & Homner 2020). Points/badges/leaderboards mainly raise *performance quantity* as extrinsic incentives; they do **not** reliably raise intrinsic motivation and sometimes lower it.
3. **Self-monitoring with no goal and no judgement is a real, evidence-backed behaviour-change mechanism** — close to what this app's "count facts only" design does — though the strongest quantified effects come *with* goals, which the app forbids.
4. **The activity is its own reward.** Musical groove/entrainment engages motor and reward circuitry (nucleus accumbens, caudate, putamen) and produces pleasure and a "wanting to move" response with recorded audio and in solo play — no human partner or performance verdict required.
5. **The removed mechanics carry the largest documented *harms*, not just the largest engagement.** Streaks, leaderboards and uncertain rewards are the mechanics most linked to anxiety, guilt, social-comparison harm and (for loot boxes) problem gambling.
6. **The single biggest risk in this design is the "return" problem**: need-supportive designs win on *long-horizon* retention and wellbeing but underperform on *short-horizon* re-engagement, which is normally rescued by exactly the reproachful/streak/notification mechanics this product has banned.

## Details, Organised by Mechanism

### 1. Neural and reinforcement mechanisms

**Dopamine, reward-prediction error, and the "wanting/liking" distinction — STRONG evidence.**
Wolfram Schultz's work established that midbrain dopamine neurons signal a *reward-prediction error* (RPE): they fire above baseline for better-than-predicted outcomes, stay at baseline for fully predicted rewards, and dip for worse-than-predicted outcomes (Schultz 2016, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*; Schultz 2016, *Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience*). Kent Berridge's incentive-salience research is the crucial corrective to pop "dopamine" writing: dopamine is "neither necessary nor sufficient" for hedonic *"liking"*; its chief causal role is *"wanting"* — the motivational pull toward reward cues (Berridge 2007, *Psychopharmacology*, 2,000+ citations).

**Grade of the "dopamine hit" framing: WEAK / folklore.** The popular claim that good game design delivers "dopamine hits" of pleasure is scientifically inaccurate. Dopamine is about anticipation and pursuit, not enjoyment. This matters directly for design ethics: mechanics engineered to maximise "wanting" (variable rewards, cliffhangers, near-misses) can drive compulsive use that users do *not* enjoy and later regret — the profile of obsessive rather than harmonious engagement (Przybylski, Weinstein, Ryan & Rigby 2009 found low need-satisfaction predicts obsessive passion, more hours, greater post-play tension, and *lower* enjoyment). Note the literature is itself moving: RPE alone fails to explain ramping, salience and aversive responses (Gershman et al. 2024, *Nature Neuroscience*; Kutlu et al. 2021, *Current Biology*), so even the RPE story is being generalised.

**Variable-ratio / intermittent reinforcement and reward uncertainty — STRONG for existence, but ethically loaded.** Variable-ratio schedules (unpredictable reward timing) are the reinforcement backbone of slot machines and loot boxes and produce high, persistent response rates. Reward *uncertainty* itself amplifies dopaminergic response. This is the most potent known engagement amplifier — and this product deliberately forgoes it. Honest statement of the cost: nothing in the allowed toolkit reproduces the raw pull of a variable-ratio schedule. What the product *can* ethically borrow is *epistemic* uncertainty (discovery of new content/toques) rather than *reward* uncertainty tied to performance.

**Loss aversion, endowed progress, goal-gradient — MODERATE, and this is why streaks work.** Streaks convert a neutral activity into a loss-framed one: missing a day *destroys* an accumulated asset, and loss aversion makes people work harder to avoid loss than to seek equivalent gain. Duolingo's own (company-published, not peer-reviewed) A/B data report "statistically significant increases in Day-1, Day-7 and Day-14 user retention, with Day-7 retention showing the greatest improvement at +14%" from a Streak Wager mechanic (Duolingo blog), and they attribute a large share of retention to streaks. **Grade: the mechanism is well-established; the specific magnitude claims are vendor-reported and not independently peer-reviewed.** This product forbids streaks — see §10 for why that is defensible on harm grounds.

**Zeigarnik effect (unfinished-task tension) — CONTESTED / WEAK.** The classic claim that interrupted tasks are remembered better and create motivating tension has a poor replication record; treat it as folklore, not a foundation.

### 2. Self-determination theory and intrinsic motivation — STRONG, and the spine of this product

SDT (Deci & Ryan) holds that motivation and wellbeing depend on satisfying three basic needs: **autonomy, competence, relatedness.** In games specifically, Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski (2006, *Motivation and Emotion*, 3,000+ citations) showed across four studies that in-game autonomy and competence predict enjoyment, preference and future play; relatedness independently adds to it. This is the **Player Experience of Need Satisfaction (PENS)** model. The key durability claim: **need satisfaction predicts sustained play and post-play wellbeing, whereas sheer hours predict neither** — and when play is driven by *low* need-satisfaction it becomes obsessive and wellbeing-negative (Przybylski et al. 2009).

**The overjustification / undermining effect — STRONG (meta-analytic).** Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999, *Psychological Bulletin*, meta-analysis of 128 experiments): tangible, expected, performance-contingent rewards significantly *undermine* free-choice intrinsic motivation. Effect sizes: engagement-contingent d=−0.40, completion-contingent d=−0.36, performance-contingent d=−0.28; all tangible rewards and all expected rewards undermined interest. Crucially, **positive verbal feedback *enhanced* intrinsic motivation** (free-choice behaviour d=+0.33; interest d=+0.31). The conditions that damage intrinsic motivation are exactly the extrinsic, judgemental, contingent rewards this product has removed; the condition that helps (informational positive feedback from a valued source) is exactly what the monthly master's correction provides. **This is the single most important result in the report for validating the product's thesis.** The effect was historically contested (Cameron & Pierce 1994 argued it was trivial) but Deci et al. rebutted this and it is now the consensus position.

**Does removing scores/points help or hurt long-term retention?** Direct evidence is thin, but the balance says removing extrinsic rewards protects intrinsic motivation *provided* competence and autonomy are supported by other means. The risk is not the removal per se; it is failing to replace the *competence signal* that points normally carry (see §11f).

### 3. Gamification meta-analyses and effect sizes — MODERATE overall

| Element / finding | Effect size | Source & grade |
|---|---|---|
| Gamification on cognitive learning | g=0.49 (95% CI 0.30–0.69, k=19) | Sailer & Homner 2020 — STRONG (meta-analysis) |
| Gamification on motivation | g=0.36 (0.18–0.54, k=16) | Sailer & Homner 2020 — STRONG |
| Gamification on behaviour | g=0.25 (0.04–0.46, k=9) | Sailer & Homner 2020 — STRONG but least stable |
| Gamified cognitive training on motivation/engagement | g=0.72 | Vermeir et al. 2020 — MODERATE (only 9 RCTs) |
| Gamification on intrinsic motivation | g=0.26 (small) | Li et al. 2024 — MODERATE |
| …on autonomy | g=0.64 | Li et al. 2024 |
| …on relatedness | g=1.78 (very wide CI) | Li et al. 2024 — WEAK (huge heterogeneity) |
| …on competence | g=0.28 (minimal) | Li et al. 2024 |
| Points/levels/leaderboards | ↑ performance *quantity*; **no** effect on intrinsic motivation or competence-need satisfaction | Mekler et al. 2017 — MODERATE (single well-designed experiment) |
| Badges, leaderboards, performance graphs | ↑ competence-need satisfaction + task meaningfulness | Mekler et al. 2017 |
| Avatars, meaningful stories, teammates | ↑ relatedness | Mekler et al. 2017 |

**What actually drives the effect:** Sailer & Homner found game *fiction* (narrative) and *social interaction* — especially competition combined with collaboration — were the significant moderators of behavioural outcomes, **not** points/badges in isolation. Mekler et al. 2017 concluded points/levels/leaderboards "functioned as extrinsic incentives, effective only for promoting performance quantity." Van Roy & Zaman (2019, *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies*) add the critical nuance that a *single* element (e.g., a leaderboard) is **motivationally ambivalent** — it can satisfy competence while simultaneously *thwarting* autonomy or relatedness, depending on the individual.

**Novelty effect and decay — MODERATE.** Gamification effects commonly fade. A longitudinal CS1 study (Rodrigues et al. 2022, N=756, Brazilian STEM students) found gamification's impact followed a U-shape: it began declining after ~4 weeks, kept falling for 2–6 weeks, then partially recovered between weeks 6–10 (a "familiarization effect"). Tsay et al. (2020, *Journal of Computer Assisted Learning*) showed that designs anchored in *intrinsic/pedagogical* need-satisfaction could overcome the novelty drop-off, whereas purely extrinsic elements decayed. **Implication:** the mechanics this product bans are the ones that decay fastest; the ones it keeps are the ones shown to be durable.

**Van Roy & Zaman's longitudinal need-supportive study (2018, *Computers & Education*, real university course):** need-supportive gamification saw **autonomous motivation *increase* over the semester**, and motivation followed a *curvilinear (polynomial)* trajectory rather than simple decay — but effects were highly individual ("one size does not fit all"). Grade: MODERATE (single ecological study; exact inferential statistics not extracted). Their earlier conceptual work (Van Roy & Zaman 2017, in *Serious Games and Edutainment Applications Vol. II*, Springer) sets out nine SDT-based "need-supporting" gamification heuristics.

**Where gamification backfires — MODERATE.** Hanus & Fox (2015) found a gamified course with leaderboard, badges and coins produced *lower* intrinsic motivation, satisfaction, empowerment and *lower* final exam scores than the non-gamified version. Leaderboards produce embarrassment and need-frustration for low-ranked users (Bai et al. 2020); high-rankers coast (competence complacency), low-rankers persist out of ego-involvement, and *neither* motivation is intrinsic (leaderboard-position study, *Internet Research* 2023). Documented failure modes: amotivation, anxiety, social-comparison harm, cheating and gaming-the-metric.

### 4. Habit formation and behaviour change

**Habit-formation timelines — MODERATE, and widely mis-cited.** Lally et al. (2010, *European Journal of Social Psychology*, N=96, 84-day daily-diary study): median time to automaticity ~66 days, but with an enormous range (18–254 days), and roughly half of participants never reached automaticity in the study window. **The "66 days" figure is a median for the successful minority on *simple* behaviours; complex behaviours take longer.** Reassuringly for a no-reproach design, **missing a single day did not measurably impair habit formation** — which directly undercuts the necessity of streaks. Context-dependent cueing (Wood & Neal) makes habits triggered by a stable context rather than willpower.

**Implementation intentions — STRONG.** Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006, meta-analysis, 94 tests, N=8,461): "if-[situation]-then-[behaviour]" plans improve goal attainment at d=0.65 (medium-to-large). This is directly usable and ethically clean: prompting the learner to form their own plan ("After I make my morning coffee, I'll play one cycle") is autonomy-supportive and needs no judgement.

**Self-monitoring as a standalone technique — MODERATE-to-STRONG, and directly on point.** This is the crux for the app's "count facts only" model. Harkin et al. (2016, *Psychological Bulletin*, meta-analysis of 138 studies, N=19,951): prompting people to *monitor* progress promoted goal attainment at d=0.40, and the effect was **larger when progress was physically recorded**. Self-monitoring is repeatedly identified as one of the most reliably effective Behaviour Change Techniques (Michie et al.); Michie et al. (2009) found self-monitoring *combined with* at least one other control-theory technique (goal-setting, feedback) outperformed interventions without it. A separate self-monitoring meta-analysis (sedentary behaviour, 19 RCTs, N=2,800) found Hedges g=0.32 for reducing sedentary time. **The important caveat and honest limitation:** most of this evidence involves monitoring *toward a goal or standard*. The app forbids goals and standards. Pure counting with *no* target and *no* comparison is less-studied; the effect almost certainly exists but is probably **weaker** than the d≈0.40 goal-linked figure, and I found **no meta-analysis isolating target-free, comparison-free self-monitoring** as a behaviour-change lever. This is a genuine evidence gap, not a confirmed strength.

**Fogg Behaviour Model (B=MAP) — WEAK empirical status.** Widely used in practice, but it is a practitioner framework with little direct experimental validation; treat as a design heuristic, not evidence.

**Notifications/nudges — MODERATE, with decay and harm caveats.** Nudges and reminders work initially but their effect decays with repetition, and *reproachful* notifications risk reactance and guilt. The product's ban on reproach is consistent with the evidence that guilt/avoidance-based motivation ("introjected regulation") predicts *dropout* in music students (see §6).

### 5. Flow, challenge–skill balance, and difficulty

**Flow (Csikszentmihalyi) — MODERATE.** The challenge–skill balance construct is well-supported as a correlate of engagement; operationalising it in real time is harder.

**Dynamic difficulty adjustment (DDA) vs. player-chosen difficulty — CONTESTED.** DDA studies are mixed: a recent within-subjects FPS experiment found **no DDA method reliably beat static difficulty** on engagement or enjoyment. Meanwhile SDT predicts that *autonomy over difficulty* (the player choosing) should beat system-imposed difficulty, and this product's "the student always chooses when to advance" is aligned with the autonomy evidence. **The honest position:** there is no strong evidence that automated difficulty beats letting the learner choose; there *is* strong evidence that autonomy supports intrinsic motivation. The product's choice is defensible and probably optimal for motivation (if not always for learning efficiency).

**Goal orientation — MODERATE.** Mastery-approach orientation (Dweck, Elliot, Ames) predicts persistence and resilience better than performance-approach orientation. Design implication: frame progress as personal mastery, never as ranking — which the product does by construction.

**Desirable difficulties (Bjork) — STRONG for learning, with a tradeoff.** Making practice harder (spacing, interleaving, retrieval) improves long-term *learning* but often *reduces* immediate performance and can feel less pleasant — a learning-vs-engagement tradeoff a beginner-first product must manage carefully.

### 6. Music and rhythm — the product's strongest asset (STRONG neuroscience)

**Dopamine and musical pleasure — STRONG.** Salimpoor et al. (2011, *Nature Neuroscience* 14:257–262): using [11C]raclopride PET + fMRI, intense pleasure to music causes endogenous dopamine release in the striatum, with a functional dissociation — "the caudate was more involved during the anticipation and the nucleus accumbens was more involved during the experience of peak emotional responses to music." Zatorre and colleagues (Salimpoor, Zald, Zatorre et al. 2015, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*) frame musical reward as a *predictive-coding* phenomenon: pleasure arises from the interplay of expectation, prediction and outcome in sound.

**Groove and the inverted-U — STRONG and directly actionable.** Witek et al. (2014, *PLoS ONE* 9(4):e94446): across funk drum-breaks, "medium degrees of syncopation elicited the most desire to move and the most pleasure… there is an inverted U-shaped relationship between syncopation, body-movement and pleasure." This has been **repeatedly replicated** (though Senn et al. 2018 found only a negligibly weak positive relationship, R²=.010, and a 2024 study found a null effect of perceived complexity — so it is robust but not unanimous). Matthews et al. (2020, *NeuroImage*) showed medium-complexity groove engages **motor *and* reward networks (NAcc, caudate, putamen)** in solo listeners. **Design implication: there is an optimal, tunable complexity "sweet spot" for the pre-rendered levels — neither trivially simple nor overwhelming.**

**Sensorimotor synchronisation / entrainment, and the recording question — STRONG for reward, IMPORTANT dissociation for social bonding.** Solo synchronisation to a beat or a *recording* robustly produces pleasure and reward via motor–reward coupling — **no human partner required** (Matthews et al. 2020; Witek et al. 2014; Salimpoor et al. 2011). This means the core act of playing along inside the master's recorded battery is *intrinsically rewarding by itself.* **However, there is a genuine dissociation:** the *affiliation/social-bonding* payoff of synchrony appears to require a perceived *human* partner. Hove & Risen (2009, *Social Cognition*): interpersonal synchrony increases affiliation/liking, and (Experiment 3) the affiliative effects were "unique to interpersonal synchrony" — synchronising with a non-social source did not produce the bonding boost. **Implication for this product:** entraining to the recording will deliver the *hedonic/reward* benefit fully, but will **not** by itself deliver the *social-relatedness* benefit — that has to come from the real human master (§7). This is a precise, evidence-based justification for the product's dual structure.

**Deliberate practice and its limits — STRONG (and humbling).** Ericsson's deliberate-practice theory is the folk basis of most practice apps, but Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014, *Psychological Science*, meta-analysis) found deliberate practice explained only **21% of performance variance in music** (26% games, 18% sports, <1% professions). Ericsson disputed the operationalisation as not matching his 1993 definition. **Implication: structured practice matters but is far from the whole story; a practice app should not over-promise skill gains from hours logged.**

**Motivation and dropout in instrumental learning — MODERATE, and highly relevant.** About 50% of students drop out of music lessons by age 17, most quitting between 15 and 17 (Ruth & Müllensiefen 2021, *PLoS ONE* 16(11):e0259105). SDT-based work (the 2024 *Frontiers in Psychology* Austrian study, N=140) found basic-need satisfaction and parental involvement, mediated by motivation, predict dropout; **autonomous motivation protects against dropout while controlled/introjected (guilt-avoidance) motivation *predicts* it** — introjected/avoidance regulation correlated positively with dropout tendency (r=0.17), while intrinsic (r=−0.50) and identified (r=−0.33) regulation protected against it. **This is direct evidence that guilt/reproach mechanics (which the product bans) are associated with *quitting*, and that a respected teacher's involvement is protective.** I found **no peer-reviewed effectiveness evaluations of commercial practice apps (Yousician, SmartMusic, Simply Piano) meeting a high evidence bar** — this is an evidence gap.

### 7. Social and relational motivation without competition — MODERATE

**Relatedness without leaderboards.** The gamification meta-analyses show relatedness is satisfied by *avatars, story, teammates and social presence* (Mekler et al. 2017), not by comparison/ranking. The music-dropout literature independently identifies a supportive, involved teacher as protective.

**Recognition by a respected authority — MODERATE and under-quantified.** The overjustification meta-analysis (Deci et al. 1999) shows *informational positive feedback* enhances intrinsic motivation (d≈0.31–0.33). Being "seen and named" by a master is a high-status, autonomy-preserving form of this. Direct experimental quantification of "named recognition by an authority" as a retention driver is thin — treat as theoretically well-grounded but not precisely sized.

**Appointment/anticipation mechanics — MODERATE.** Anticipation of a scheduled event is a legitimate, non-coercive retention driver (and aligns with the dopaminergic *anticipation* finding, Salimpoor 2011). The monthly master's-listening cycle is an "appointment mechanic" with a human, ethical basis rather than a manufactured cliffhanger.

**Communities of practice / legitimate peripheral participation (Lave & Wenger) — THEORETICAL.** Apt for an apprenticeship framing (candomblé transmission is itself an oral/apprenticeship tradition) but this is a qualitative/theoretical lens, not an effect-sized intervention.

### 8. Curiosity, discovery, and collection — MODERATE

**Information-gap theory (Loewenstein 1994, *Psychological Bulletin*).** Curiosity is a "cognitively induced deprivation" from a perceived gap in knowledge; it peaks when you know *some* but not all — small doses of information amplify the drive. **This is the ethical substitute for variable-ratio reward:** *discovery* of new toques/levels functions as a variable *epistemic* reward that requires **no performance judgement** — you unlock the next thing by choosing to explore, not by being assessed. Collection/set-completion drives are real but under-quantified; discovery-based engagement is a legitimate, judgement-free analogue of the "one more" pull.

### 9. Session shape and stopping — MODERATE

**Peak-end rule — STRONG (recently meta-analysed).** Alaybek et al. (2022, meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes): the peak-end effect on retrospective evaluation is large (r=0.58) and robust, and **duration is essentially neglected.** Redelmeier, Katz & Kahneman (2003, *Pain* 104:187–194, RCT of N=682 outpatients, median 5.3-yr follow-up) showed a better-ending colonoscopy raised *actual return rates* for repeat screening: "Rates of returning for a repeat colonoscopy… averaged 50.4% and were slightly higher (odds ratio=1.41, P=0.038) for those who underwent the longer procedure." **Implication: how a practice session *ends* disproportionately determines whether the learner wants to return — a satisfying musical resolution or a warm spoken sign-off from the master at session end is a high-leverage, evidence-backed design.**

**Time-to-first-value / "aha moment" — MODERATE (industry-heavy).** First-session completion and rapid first value are strong predictors of D1/D7/D30 retention in app analytics. Much of this evidence is practitioner/industry data rather than peer-reviewed; treat the direction as reliable, the magnitudes as vendor-reported.

### 10. Ethics and the dark side — STRONG on harm

**Loot boxes and problem gambling — STRONG (but correlational).** More than a dozen studies plus multiple meta-analyses (Zendle & Cairns 2018, 2019; Drummond et al. 2020; Spicer et al. 2021) find loot-box spending is **robustly correlated with problem-gambling severity** (η²≈0.05; subgroup Cohen's d≈0.28–0.57). The direction of causation is unknown (a limitation the authors stress). These are variable-ratio, uncertainty-based purchase mechanics — the extreme end of the "wanting" exploitation this product refuses.

**Dark patterns (Zagal et al.; Deterding et al.) — MODERATE.** Mechanics engineered against user interest (manufactured scarcity, guilt, sunk-cost traps) are catalogued as unethical game design.

**Streak loss and anxiety — MODERATE/EMERGING.** Streaks drive retention via loss aversion but the same mechanism produces guilt, anxiety, and "streak grief"; the music-dropout evidence links guilt-avoidance motivation to quitting. Even Duolingo has publicly moved toward softening streak loss (streak-repair, "earn back") — an implicit admission of the harm.

**"Humane"/eudaimonic design and whether it costs engagement — MIXED.** SDT-based, need-supportive designs win on wellbeing and long-horizon retention (Van Roy & Zaman 2018; Tsay et al. 2020) but often lose on short-horizon engagement metrics. There *is* a real short-term cost; the claim that humane design is "free" is not supported.

**Grading popular frameworks:**
- **Octalysis (Chou) — FOLKLORE.** A useful practitioner taxonomy with essentially no independent peer-reviewed validation. Use as an idea-generation checklist, not evidence.
- **Hooked model (Eyal) — FOLKLORE / practitioner theory.** The trigger-action-variable-reward-investment loop is a repackaging of operant conditioning; it has descriptive value but no direct empirical validation as a model, and its variable-reward core is the ethically problematic part this product rejects.
- **Fogg B=MAP — WEAK** (see §4).
- **PENS/SDT — STRONG.** The one practitioner-facing framework with a deep empirical base.

### 11. Synthesis for the hard-constrained product

**(a) Which evidence-backed mechanics remain available, ranked by strength of evidence and expected effect size:**

| Rank | Mechanic (allowed) | Evidence grade | Expected effect | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Intrinsic reward of the activity (groove/entrainment)** | STRONG | Large, durable | Salimpoor 2011; Witek 2014; Matthews 2020 |
| 2 | **Need support (autonomy: self-paced advance; competence: mastery framing; relatedness: the master)** | STRONG | Moderate-large, durable | Ryan/Rigby/Przybylski 2006; Deci et al. 1999 |
| 3 | **Relatedness + recognition via the named master ("appointment")** | MODERATE-STRONG | Moderate-large (protective vs dropout) | Music-dropout SDT studies; Deci et al. 1999 |
| 4 | **Peak-end session design (satisfying endings)** | STRONG | Moderate on return rate | Alaybek 2022; Redelmeier 2003 |
| 5 | **Self-monitoring / pure counting** | MODERATE (STRONG for goal-linked) | Small-moderate (d~0.4 with goals; likely less without) | Harkin 2016 |
| 6 | **Implementation intentions / cue design** | STRONG | Medium (d=0.65) | Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006 |
| 7 | **Curiosity / discovery of new content (epistemic variable reward)** | MODERATE | Small-moderate | Loewenstein 1994 |
| 8 | **Optimal challenge / autonomy over difficulty** | MODERATE | Small-moderate | Flow lit; DDA-vs-static evidence |

**(b) The measured cost of forgoing streaks, targets, points, leaderboards and progress bars.** The honest answer: **real but smaller than practitioners assume.** Points/leaderboards mainly raise performance *quantity* as extrinsic incentives and do **not** reliably build intrinsic motivation (Mekler 2017); their effects **decay fastest** under the novelty effect (Rodrigues 2022) and sometimes go **negative** (Hanus & Fox 2015). Streaks are the biggest genuine loss — Duolingo's (vendor-reported, non-peer-reviewed) figures suggest streaks drive a large share of retention (e.g., ~+14% Day-7 in one experiment). But those figures come from a product with *no intrinsic reward comparable to making music*, and the mechanics carry documented harm. Net: the product forgoes perhaps the top *short-term* retention lever (streaks) and several weak/decaying ones (points, leaderboards, progress bars), while keeping every mechanic the literature associates with *durable* motivation.

**(c) Does need-supportive design beat extrinsic gamification on long-horizon retention even where it loses short-term?** **Yes, the evidence leans this way.** Need-supported motivation predicts sustained play and wellbeing where hours/extrinsic rewards do not (Przybylski 2009); need-supportive gamification grew autonomous motivation over a semester (Van Roy & Zaman 2018); intrinsic/pedagogically-anchored designs overcame the novelty decay that killed extrinsic designs (Tsay 2020); and the overjustification meta-analysis shows extrinsic rewards can *undermine* the very motivation you need for the long haul (Deci et al. 1999). The tradeoff is real: you likely lose on Day-1/Day-7 engagement metrics and win on 3–12-month retention and wellbeing.

**(d) Self-monitoring without goals — pure counting.** Best available anchor: Harkin et al. 2016 (d=0.40 for progress monitoring, stronger when physically recorded). **But** that literature almost always involves a goal/standard, which this app forbids. Target-free, comparison-free counting is under-studied; the effect plausibly exists (awareness alone changes behaviour) but is likely **smaller** than d=0.40, and **no meta-analysis isolates it.** Flag this as the product's biggest *evidence gap* — worth measuring in-house.

**(e) Does the rhythm/groove literature imply less need for extrinsic mechanics than a language app?** **Yes — this is the strongest strategic finding.** Making rhythmic music is *intrinsically* rewarding at the neural level (dopaminergic reward from groove/entrainment, achievable solo with a recording), whereas conjugating verbs is not. A language app must *manufacture* reward (streaks, XP, leagues) precisely because the core activity is not intrinsically pleasurable for most users; a percussion app is playing on a home field where the activity supplies its own "wanting to move" and pleasure. This materially lowers the marginal value of extrinsic scaffolding here relative to Duolingo.

**(f) Biggest risks and blind spots — where such products actually fail:**
1. **The return problem (highest risk).** Need-supportive, no-notification, no-streak designs underperform on short-horizon re-engagement. With human contact only monthly and no reproach, a beginner who wavers on Day 3 has *nothing* pulling them back. This is exactly the gap streaks/notifications normally fill. **Mitigation:** implementation intentions at onboarding (d=0.65), gentle non-reproachful cue reminders, peak-end session design so each session leaves a positive trace, and shortening the felt distance to the next master interaction.
2. **The competence-signal vacuum.** Removing points *and* having no microphone means the app **cannot tell the learner they are improving.** Competence is a core need (Ryan 2006) and self-efficacy protects against music dropout. With no assessment and no scores, perceived competence may starve. **Mitigation:** let *the activity itself* signal competence (the learner can hear themselves locking into the groove), scaffold complexity in the groove sweet spot (Witek 2014) so success is felt, and use the master's positive informational feedback (Deci d≈0.33) as the competence anchor.
3. **Novelty decay without replenishment.** Even intrinsic designs need fresh content; discovery (§8) must be continuously fed.
4. **Over-reliance on the monthly appointment.** If the master interaction is the primary relatedness/competence source and it is only monthly, the between-cycle void is dangerous. Consider more frequent low-cost "presence" (his recorded voice, count-ins, spoken calls) to sustain parasocial relatedness between cycles.
5. **Evidence gaps you are betting on:** target-free self-monitoring (unquantified), named-recognition-as-retention-driver (unquantified), and the absence of any peer-reviewed practice-app effectiveness benchmark. These are reasonable bets grounded in adjacent evidence, but they are bets — instrument them.

---

## Recommendations (staged, with thresholds)

**Stage 1 — Build on the strongest evidence first (do these regardless):**
1. **Maximise the intrinsic reward of playing.** Tune the pre-rendered levels toward the groove complexity sweet spot (medium syncopation; Witek 2014) so entrainment feels good from session one. This is your #1 asset.
2. **Design every session with a peak-end.** End on a musically satisfying resolution and/or a warm spoken word from the master. Evidence: Alaybek 2022 (r=0.58), Redelmeier 2003 (return-rate OR 1.41). High leverage, low cost.
3. **Onboard with an implementation intention.** Prompt the learner to write their own if-then plan ("After [existing daily habit], I'll play one cycle"). Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, d=0.65. Autonomy-preserving.
4. **Make the master's voice frequent, not monthly.** Use recorded count-ins, calls and named encouragement throughout, to sustain relatedness between listening cycles.

**Stage 2 — Fill the competence and return gaps:**
5. **Engineer felt competence without judgement:** scaffold difficulty so the learner *hears* themselves succeed; frame all master feedback as informational and positive (Deci d≈0.33).
6. **Add discovery as ethical variable reward:** let new toques/levels be *discovered* by exploration, not earned by assessment (Loewenstein 1994).
7. **Non-reproachful cueing only:** optional reminders framed as invitations ("your battery is ready"), never "you've fallen behind." Guilt-avoidance motivation predicts dropout (introjected regulation r=0.17 with dropout tendency).

**Stage 3 — Measure the bets:**
8. Instrument D1/D7/D30 retention and, critically, **3-, 6- and 12-month** retention (where need-supportive design should win).
9. **Run an in-house test of target-free self-monitoring**: does surfacing "minutes/cycles played" (pure counts, no target) change return behaviour vs. hiding it? This is your key evidence gap (Harkin 2016 only covers goal-linked monitoring).

**Thresholds that would change the recommendation:**
- If **Day-2–Day-7 return rate is catastrophically low** (e.g., <15–20% return by Day 7) despite Stage 1–2, the return problem is dominating; consider adding an *opt-in*, self-set, losable-by-nobody "practice rhythm" goal (autonomy-supportive, not a streak) before conceding to reproachful mechanics.
- If **long-horizon (3–12 month) retention is strong** even with weak D1/D7, the thesis is validated — hold the line and do **not** add extrinsic mechanics chasing short-term metrics.
- If **in-house self-monitoring test shows null effect**, drop or de-emphasise the counters rather than letting them imply a hidden standard.

## Caveats
- **Correlational vs. causal:** The music-dropout and loot-box/gambling findings are largely *correlational* (the authors say so). Salimpoor 2011 is a causal neuroimaging design. The overjustification, implementation-intention, self-monitoring, peak-end and gamification meta-analyses are built on *experimental* studies and support causal claims; Redelmeier 2003 is a randomised trial.
- **Effect-size heterogeneity is high** across all gamification meta-analyses (I² frequently >85%), so pooled estimates are directional, not precise.
- **Vendor data (Duolingo, app-analytics "aha moment" claims) are not peer-reviewed** and are cited as such; treat magnitudes sceptically.
- **Genuine evidence gaps:** (1) target-free, comparison-free self-monitoring; (2) named-recognition-by-an-authority as a quantified retention driver; (3) any high-quality peer-reviewed effectiveness study of a commercial music-practice app; (4) entrainment-with-a-recording specifically for *sustained retention* (the reward finding is solid; the retention link is inferred). None should be filled with assumption — they should be measured.
- **Contested/failed-to-replicate items flagged in text:** Zeigarnik effect (weak/contested); the "dopamine hit" framing (inaccurate); the groove inverted-U (robust but with ≥2 null/weak studies — Senn et al. 2018, R²=.010; a 2024 null study); DDA-beats-static (not established); Fogg/Octalysis/Hooked (practitioner folklore without independent validation).
